The US patent journey

Six stages. Tap any to explore.

Every patent follows a path. See what happens at each stage, how long it usually takes, and where a second pair of eyes helps.

Stage 1 of 6

Idea and prior-art check

Before you spend on a filing, learn what has already been published and where your approach is different.

What happens
We talk through how the technology works, then look at patents and papers that come close.
Usual timing
Ideally before any public demo, paper, pitch or launch.
How we help
A patentability and prior-art search that turns patent data into a clear recommendation.
Patentability & prior art
Stage 2 of 6

Provisional application

A provisional application secures an early filing date for what it describes while you keep building.

What happens
A technical disclosure is filed. It is not examined and never becomes a patent by itself.
Usual timing
It expires after 12 months, so a full application has to follow within that window.
How we help
A disclosure written around your real invention, with alternatives and ranges that support later claims.
Provisional applications
Stage 3 of 6

Non-provisional application

The full application, with claims, is what the US patent office examines.

What happens
The specification, drawings and claims are filed so the USPTO can examine the invention.
Usual timing
Filed within 12 months of a provisional if you want to keep its filing date.
How we help
Claims and a specification shaped around the product, the market and likely questions from an examiner.
US patent prosecution
Stage 4 of 6

Examination

An examiner compares your claims with the prior art and decides what can be allowed.

What happens
The application waits in a queue, then an examiner reviews it and reports back.
Usual timing
Timing varies with the technology area and the patent office's workload.
How we help
We keep the technology, the business plan and the budget in view while the application is examined.
US patent prosecution
Stage 5 of 6

Office action and response

Most applications receive objections or rejections first. A good response is part of the process, not the end of it.

What happens
The examiner issues a written action. You respond with arguments, amended claims or an interview.
Usual timing
The action sets a response deadline, commonly three months and extendable for a fee.
How we help
Responses that protect what matters most and explain the technology clearly to the examiner.
US patent prosecution
Stage 6 of 6

Allowance and grant

When claims are allowed and fees are paid, the patent is granted. Keeping it and using it well comes next.

What happens
The patent issues. Granted US utility patents need maintenance fees to stay in force.
Usual timing
Maintenance fees fall due about 3.5, 7.5 and 11.5 years after grant.
How we help
Portfolio planning that stands up to investor questions, product decisions and growth.
